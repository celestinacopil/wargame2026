Aldeia / Cyber / Challenge 05

What the satellite saw

Available

Instructions

An operation like the attack on the Aurora needs orders. Aldeian intelligence believes Belvaria did not send them through its own military channels. Instead, it rented the messaging service of a private, civilian satellite, so that nothing would point back to the Belvarian state.

Aldeian intelligence has got hold of image files that this satellite sent down to Earth on 2 December 2026. HQ will not say how. The files are on a forensic workstation.

The satellite is a radar satellite (also called SAR). In December the Arctic is in polar night: the sun does not rise, and an ordinary camera sees nothing. A radar satellite sends out its own radio signal and records the echo, so it can see in the dark and through clouds. Ships show up as bright dots on the dark sea.

Like phone photos, satellite images carry hidden data called metadata. Use the workstation's exiftool command to read it. Find the image taken at the time of the source's account in challenge 03 (2 December, around 21:10 UTC), then:

  1. find out which satellite took it and who operates it. This is not part of the flag, but note it down: it tells HQ whose records to go after.
  2. find the position of the satellite (not of the ships) when it took the image. If the satellite was overhead at that moment, it could also have relayed orders to the ships.

Flag format: latitude and longitude in decimal degrees, rounded to 2 decimals: FLAG{latitude,longitude}, for example FLAG{12.34,56.78}

Evidence

  • Forensic workstation (terminal with the recovered satellite images)
  • View the recovered radar image of 2 December, evening (original file)

Investigation guidance

  • New to the terminal? Type help, then ls to see the files. Read the evidence note with cat.
  • There is more than one image. Check when each was taken: exiftool -DateTimeOriginal *.jpg shows the date of every image at once.
  • Not every copy of an image keeps its metadata. Email and messaging apps often strip it when they make an image smaller.
  • A satellite flies about 500 km up, so its radio can reach ships far below it, not only the ones directly underneath.
  • Metadata uses camera words even for a satellite. Think about what "Make" and "Camera Model Name" mean when the camera is in space.
  • A radar satellite does not look straight down: it looks sideways, so it can be hundreds of kilometres from what it photographs. Read every tag. One gives the centre of the picture, another gives the satellite's own position. Only one of them is the answer.
  • GPS coordinates are shown in degrees, minutes and seconds by default. Convert them to decimal degrees (degrees + minutes/60 + seconds/3600), or check man exiftool for an option that does it for you.
  • Does the image agree with challenge 03? Look up where the Aurora and the vessel you identified were at 21:10 in VesselTrace, and compare with the centre of the picture. How many bright dots can you see?
